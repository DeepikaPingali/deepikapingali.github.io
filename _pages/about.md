I am a Ph.D. Candidate in Agricultural and Applied Economics at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

I am a networks economist. I study how connectedness, both social and spatial, shapes the choices and socioeconomic trajectories of households, including under economic and environmental stress. My work centers on household labor decisions, from migration to the intrahousehold division of labor. I work with a wide quantitative toolkit spanning structural identification, social network analysis, machine learning, geospatial analysis, and causal inference, applied across a range of contexts in South Asia, the Middle East, and Latin America.

Before my Ph.D., I spent over five years in the international development sector across South Asia, directing grassroots programs, founding a women's collective-entrepreneurship venture, and advising organizations including the World Bank, the ILO, and UNDP. That experience continues to shape the questions I ask and the communities my research centers.

## Job Market Paper

***Uncovering the Influence of Hidden Social Networks on Gendered Labor Norms: Evidence from Rural India***

Economics has traditionally characterized spousal labor sharing as the outcome of an intrahousehold bargaining process; however, these decisions are also shaped by norms embedded within and transmitted through social networks. This paper studies the influence of peer behavior on spouses’ relative contributions to domestic and production labor in rural India. A key empirical challenge is that social network ties are unobserved in most existing datasets. I overcome this challenge by jointly estimating the latent social structure, peer effects, and contextual effects within a linear social interactions model. I use an Adaptive Elastic Net Generalized Method of Moments, recovering network architecture without requiring directly observed network data. Using the Village Dynamics in South Asia (VDSA) dataset across 16 villages and a balanced panel of 629 households, I find that within-sample peer networks governing production and domestic labor-sharing differ in topology. Both, domestic labor sharing and production labor sharing exhibit a statistically significant social multiplier. A 10%  increase in peers’ relative male-to-female domestic work leads to a 6.2% increase in a couple's own relative male-to-female domestic work. Similarly, a 10% increase in peers’ relative female-to-male production work leads to a 9.3% increase in a couple's own relative female-to-male production work. Exploratory analysis of the recovered networks shows that couples maintain influence ties with roughly five peers on average, and that this degree is stable across village sizes. I also examine assortativity, the tendency to connect with similar peers. Domestic-norm networks are positively religion- and land-assortative, meaning same-religion and similarly landed couples are tied more often than chance would predict; production-norm networks show no such assortativity on either dimension. Women's participation in self-help groups is positively associated with centrality in production-norm networks, whereas landlessness is negatively associated with it. In contrast, higher centrality in domestic-norm networks is associated with a larger spousal age gap, greater female education, and lower-to-middle landholding status. My findings provide novel insights and evidence on how network-mediated spillover effects are integral in shaping intrahousehold labor distributions.
